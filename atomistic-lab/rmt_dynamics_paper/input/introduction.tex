\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Time-correlation functions connect microscopic dynamics to macroscopic observables.
Through the Green--Kubo relations~\cite{green54a,kubo57a,kubo66a}, equilibrium fluctuations determine transport coefficients, whose symmetries follow from Onsager's reciprocal relations~\cite{onsager31a,onsager31b}, and through van Hove functions they are tied to scattering experiments~\cite{hansen90a}.
In practice, however, this information is compressed before it is used: a viscosity or an ionic conductivity is reported as a scalar, at most split into a Nernst--Einstein baseline and a distinct-correlation correction written down for that one coefficient~\cite{kashyap11a,fong20a,fong21a}.
Working back from such a coefficient to its microscopic definition recovers a sum over pairs of atoms of integrated velocity cross-correlations.
The object behind that sum, an $|\N| \times |\N|$ real symmetric matrix over all atoms, is seldom examined on its own terms.

Correlation matrices built from high-dimensional data are studied extensively elsewhere, where random matrix theory (RMT) separates physical structure from finite-sampling noise~\cite{plerou1999,laloux1999,bouchaud2011}.
In molecular simulation the closest relatives are configurational: Schlitter's entropy~\cite{schlitter1993,andricioaei2001} and essential-dynamics analyses~\cite{amadei1993} diagonalise the positional covariance matrix, dynamic cross-correlation maps~\cite{ichiye1991} display its normalised elements, and Hess showed that the leading principal components of such matrices can be indistinguishable from those of random diffusion~\cite{hess2000,hess2002}, a cautionary result that motivates comparison with a noise null.
The matrix of integrated velocity cross-correlations, which is the one that enters the Green--Kubo decomposition of transport coefficients, has to our knowledge not been treated with the same spectral tools.

Here we do so in two steps.
First, we write any transport coefficient of the form $\lambda \propto \int_{0}^{\infty} dt\, \langle A(t) \cdot A(0) \rangle$, with $A = \sum_{i} \eta_{\alpha(i)} a_{i}$ built from a single-particle primitive $a_{i}$ and a species-specific scalar $\eta_{\alpha}$, as a linear functional of one matrix, the atom-wise integrated correlation matrix $C$.
This puts self-diffusion, ionic conductivity and electrophoretic mobility on a common footing.
Second, we study $C$ itself, through its eigenvalue spectrum, its eigenvectors and its spectral entropy, measured against a Marchenko--Pastur null whose sample count is set by the integration lag.
Using a Lennard-Jones liquid with controlled bond addition, a Lennard-Jones system across its phases, water and molten NaCl, we show that bonds remove eigenvalues from the noise bulk through its lower edge, one per independent bounded relative coordinate, with eigenvectors that reproduce the bonding topology once the lag exceeds the time for a pair to diffuse over its bond length; that the entropy of $C$ measured against the null distinguishes solid from liquid, while in solids it reflects vibrational displacement covariance rather than transport; and that in molten NaCl the departure of the spectrum from the null grows with the distinct correlation that sets the Nernst--Einstein deviation of the ionic conductivity.

Section~\ref{sec:theory} develops the decomposition, the matrix $C$ and its spectral observables, Section~\ref{sec:methods} the estimator, the noise null and the simulations, Section~\ref{sec:experiments} the results, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} summarises what the spectrum of $C$ does and does not resolve.
