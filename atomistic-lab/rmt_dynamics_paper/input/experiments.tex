\subsection{Validation of the Noise Null}
\label{subsec:convergence-of-the-noise-term}
Before turning to interacting systems we check the null of Section~\ref{subsec:de-noising-correlation-matrices} on a system with no correlation structure at all: $|\N| = 256$ independent Ornstein--Uhlenbeck velocity processes with a correlation time of $5$ sampling intervals, $2 \times 10^{4}$ samples long, for which every off-diagonal element of $C$ is sampling noise.
With the lag-window sample count of Equation~\ref{eqn:teff}, the Marchenko--Pastur density describes the spectrum at every lag from $2$ to $20$ correlation times (Figure~\ref{fig:noise-null}): the Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance stays between $0.011$ and $0.029$, the largest eigenvalue lies within $1\%$ of the upper edge, and no more than one of the $256$ eigenvalues falls outside the bulk.
The naive count $T_{\text{traj}}/\tau_{\text{int}}$ ignores the lag and fails increasingly as the lag grows: at $\tau_{\max} = 10\,\tau_{\text{int}}$ it places $30$ eigenvalues above and $40$ below a bulk that is too narrow, with a Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance of $0.18$, and at $20\,\tau_{\text{int}}$ the distance reaches $0.29$.
Every count of outliers in what follows uses the lag-window sample count.

\subsection{Bond Addition}
\label{subsec:bond-addition}
To test how bonding enters the spectrum under controlled conditions, we add $k$ harmonic bonds to an equilibrated Lennard-Jones liquid at $T = 80$~K and $\rho = 0.8\,\sigma^{-3}$ and compare against the same liquid restarted without bonds (Figure~\ref{fig:renderings}a).
Two protocols are used.
In the first, $|\N| = 512$, bonds join random pairs closer than $2\sigma$, each run is $1$~ns long and $C$ is evaluated with $\tau_{\max} = 2$~ps ($T_{\text{eff}} \approx 2260$, $q \approx 0.23$).
In the second, chosen to satisfy the lag condition of Section~\ref{subsec:interpreting-atomic-correlation-matrices}, $|\N| = 216$, bonds are restricted to the first coordination shell ($r_{0} < 1.3\,\sigma$), each run is $26$~ns long and $\tau_{\max} = 100$~ps ($T_{\text{eff}} \approx 1170$, $q \approx 0.18$).
All quantities are averaged over three seeds; Figure~\ref{fig:bonding} collects the results.

At $\tau_{\max} = 2$~ps the bond-free spectrum is bulk-like (Figure~\ref{fig:bonding}a): a single eigenvalue sits at zero, as required by conservation of total momentum, and $11$ to $12$ of the $512$ eigenvalues lie above the upper edge.
These upper modes are delocalised, with participation ratios near $170$, and are present before any bond is added, so they are not a bonding signature.
Adding bonds produces no new localised modes above the bulk; instead, weight moves towards and through the lower edge, as anticipated in Section~\ref{subsec:interpreting-atomic-correlation-matrices}.

The effect of a bond is unambiguous at the level of a single pair.
The relative-motion mode of a bonded pair is $0.61 \pm 0.01$ of the free value at $k = 64$ and $0.52 \pm 0.01$ at $k = 256$, against $0.99 \pm 0.04$ for unbonded pairs.
This reduction is not large enough to carry the mode out of the bulk, whose lower edge lies at $0.28$ in the same units, so the number of sub-bulk eigenvalues lags far behind the number of constraints (Figure~\ref{fig:bonding}d and Table~\ref{tab:bond-addition}): it is zero at $k = 16$, reaches $53$ of $254$ at $k = 256$, and approaches the constraint count only at $k = 1024$, where the bond network spans almost the whole system and diffusion is largely arrested.
The lowest eigenvectors nonetheless carry the bond topology before individual modes separate, capturing $18\%$ and $37\%$ of the constraint space at $k = 16$ and $64$, six and three times the value for random modes (Figure~\ref{fig:bonding}e).

The incomplete separation at short lags is a consequence of the lag, not of the estimator.
A bond fixes the separation of a pair but not its orientation, so its relative coordinate is distinguishable from free diffusion only when $\tau_{\max} \gg r_{0}^{2}/6D$; with $D \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-4}$~\AA$^{2}$\,fs$^{-1}$ and $r_{0}$ up to $2\sigma$, this requires tens of picoseconds.
Re-evaluating one $16$-bond trajectory with longer lags confirms the trend (Figure~\ref{fig:bonding}c): the bonded mode falls from $0.61$ at $2$~ps to $0.39$ at $40$~ps while unbonded pairs stay at $0.98$--$0.99$, but beyond $10$~ps a $1$~ns trajectory has too few independent samples for the bulk edge to be defined.

The second protocol meets both conditions, and the bond signature becomes spectral as well as pair-wise (Figure~\ref{fig:bonding}b).
The bonded relative-motion mode falls to $0.13$--$0.15$ of the free value, well below the lower bulk edge at $0.33$.
In all three seeds exactly $8$ and $27$ eigenvalues leave the bulk through its lower edge for $8$ and $27$ bonds, and $51$--$52$ do so for $54$ bonds; at $k = 108$, where bonded atoms make up most of the system and the diffusive baseline shifts, the count is $91$--$95$ for $106$--$108$ constraints (Figure~\ref{fig:bonding}d).
The corresponding eigenvectors reproduce the bond graph, capturing $86$--$94\%$ of the constraint space against $4$--$25\%$ for random modes (Figure~\ref{fig:bonding}e), and Figure~\ref{fig:renderings}a shows that their weight sits on the bonded atoms.
The bond-free liquid has a single eigenvalue above the upper edge in this regime, compared with $11$ to $12$ at $2$~ps.

In both protocols the von Neumann entropy decreases monotonically with the number of bonds (Figure~\ref{fig:bonding}f), from $6.1133 \pm 0.0005$ to $5.697 \pm 0.007$ at $\tau_{\max} = 2$~ps and from $5.2791 \pm 0.0005$ to $5.0448 \pm 0.0013$ at $100$~ps; at $2$~ps the $16$-bond system is separated from the bond-free liquid by about three times the seed scatter.
Bonding is therefore read off the spectrum of $C$ as a count of sub-bulk eigenvalues, one per constraint, and off its eigenvectors as the bonding topology, provided the lag exceeds the time a free pair needs to diffuse over the bond length; at shorter lags the same information is present pair by pair but does not separate from the noise bulk.

\subsection{Lennard-Jones Phases}
\label{subsec:lennard-jones-phases}
We next follow $C$ across the solid, liquid and vapour phases of a single-component Lennard-Jones system with argon parameters, $|\N| = 500$, along the $P = 20$~atm isobar from $20$ to $160$~K.
The pressure is chosen to be below the critical pressure but high enough that the liquid is stable over several tens of kelvin; at $1$~atm the liquid range is only a few kelvin.
Each temperature is simulated from an fcc crystal, from a dense disordered configuration and from a dilute gas, with three seeds each, so that the hysteresis of the first-order transitions in a $500$-atom box is visible rather than hidden; the protocol is given in Section~\ref{subsec:molecular-dynamics-simulations}.
$C$ is built with $\tau_{\max} = 2$~ps.

Because $T_{\text{eff}}$, and hence $q$, changes by an order of magnitude across the isobar, the raw entropy $S(C)$ mixes physical structure with the sampling noise of Section~\ref{subsec:de-noising-correlation-matrices}: in the liquid, for example, $S(C)/\ln|\N|$ falls from $0.9805$ to $0.9758$ between $80$ and $130$~K largely because $q$ rises from $0.21$ to $0.30$.
We therefore report the entropy deficit $\Delta S = S_{\text{MP}}(q) - S(C)$, where $S_{\text{MP}}(q)$ is the mean entropy of a white Wishart matrix of the same size and aspect ratio, evaluated numerically.
$\Delta S$ is zero for a spectrum indistinguishable from noise and positive when correlation is concentrated in fewer modes than noise alone would produce.
In the vapour $q$ exceeds one, where the moment-matched null is not reliable, and $\Delta S$ is not reported.

Figure~\ref{fig:lj-results} shows the three branches.
The crystal remains crystalline up to $90$~K and melts between $90$ and $95$~K, where its density drops from $1.542$ to $1.294$~g\,cm$^{-3}$.
The two disordered starts are liquid from $60$~K upwards and solidify between $50$ and $60$~K into a solid whose density, $1.65$--$1.70$~g\,cm$^{-3}$, is below that of the crystal at the same temperature; we did not determine whether it is amorphous or polycrystalline.
On heating, the liquid branches remain liquid up to $130$~K and vaporise by $135$~K, while the dilute start condenses only between $110$ and $115$~K, where the three seeds split between liquid and vapour.
Melting coexistence on this isobar therefore lies between $50$ and $95$~K and liquid--vapour coexistence between $110$ and $135$~K.

The entropy deficit separates the three condensed states.
In the crystal $\Delta S/\ln|\N|$ rises from $0.040$ at $20$~K to $0.064$ at $90$~K; in the disordered solid it is larger, $0.062$ to $0.074$; and in the liquid it is an order of magnitude smaller, $0.0097$ at $60$~K, decaying monotonically to $10^{-4}$ at $120$~K and to zero within the seed scatter of a few $10^{-5}$ above.
The seed-to-seed scatter in the liquid is about $10^{-4}$ or smaller, so this decay is resolved at every temperature.
The two disordered starts give identical $\Delta S$ wherever they are in the same phase, and differ from the crystal branch by a factor of about $1.5$ at the same temperature and similar density, so $\Delta S$ distinguishes the crystalline from the disordered solid as well as the solid from the liquid.

Every step in $\Delta S$ coincides with a step in density and in diffusion at the same grid point, on every branch, so the spectral and the thermodynamic observables locate the transitions at the same temperatures.
On the $5$~K grid used here both steps are complete within one interval: the full widths at half maximum of $|d\rho/dT|$ and $|dS/dT|$ at the boiling transition are $10.3$ and $10.3$~K for the dense branch and $10.3$ and $10.4$~K for the crystal branch, and $11.4$ and $10.2$~K at melting on the crystal branch, all set by the grid spacing.
These data therefore do not show that $S(C)$ resolves a transition more sharply than the density.

The spectra behind these numbers (Figure~\ref{fig:lj-results}d--f) make the difference visible.
The liquid spectrum follows the Marchenko--Pastur density closely, whereas both solids pile weight into small eigenvalues below the bulk and spread a tail of large ones beyond it, more strongly in the disordered solid than in the crystal.

The physical content of $\Delta S$ differs between the phases.
In the solids the diagonal of $C$ is two orders of magnitude smaller than in the liquid (Figure~\ref{fig:lj-results}c) and, as noted in Section~\ref{subsec:interpreting-atomic-correlation-matrices}, $C$ vanishes for a non-diffusing solid in the limit of long lags; at $\tau_{\max} = 2$~ps its structure reflects vibrational correlations that have not decayed within the window.
In the liquid the deficit reflects correlated displacements of neighbouring atoms over $2$~ps, which weaken as temperature rises until the spectrum is indistinguishable from the noise null.
$\Delta S$ is not a function of the diffusion coefficient alone: it falls by a factor of six at melting while the diffusion coefficient rises, and continues to fall within the liquid while the diffusion coefficient rises further, but the solid branches show the opposite trend, with $\Delta S$ rising alongside diffusion.

\subsection{Water Phases}
\label{subsec:water-phases}
Water adds rigid molecular structure and a hydrogen-bond network to the questions above.
We follow TIP4P/2005 water along the $1$~atm isobar from $200$ to $340$~K, from a proton-disordered ice Ih crystal and from a pre-melted liquid, so that melting near the model's melting point of $252$~K is bracketed by superheated ice and supercooled liquid in the same way as for the Lennard-Jones crystal.
$C$ is built for the oxygen atoms alone and for all atoms, which separates the motion of molecular centres from the intramolecular correlation of each oxygen with its two hydrogens.

\subsection{Molten Salt Ionic Conductivity}
\label{subsec:molten-salt-ionic-conductivity}
Molten NaCl tests the transport decomposition of Section~\ref{subsec:microscopic-origins-of-transport-properties} directly.
With velocities as the single-particle primitive and the ionic charges as $\eta_{\alpha}$, the Nernst--Einstein deviation $\Delta^{\sigma}$ of the ionic conductivity is a ratio of block sums of $C$, and must agree with the deviation obtained from the collective charge current.
We follow the Tosi--Fumi model along the $1$~atm isobar from $900$ to $1800$~K, from a rock-salt crystal and from a pre-melted liquid, which brackets melting near $1070$~K and gives the conductivity of the liquid over a wide temperature range.
